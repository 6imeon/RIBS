Three that exceed their whole target

Kensington & Chelsea — 131%

The sharpest case in London. Highest demand pressure of any borough, enough permitted-but-unstarted capacity to cover its entire ten-year target with room to spare, and a median permission that has sat for nine and a half years.

Barking & Dagenham — 120%

436 hectares, the largest area of stalled permitted land in London — 23,251 homes against a 19,440 target. Demand pressure here is low by London standards, which makes it a different argument: the land is ready, the market is not pulling.

Camden — 106%

10,973 homes permitted and unstarted on just 40 hectares — the densest stalled capacity in the city, in the fifth most pressured borough, waiting a median 8.5 years.

What this is, and is not

  • Not "all available land in London." It is land each borough has itself assessed as suitable for housing and published on its brownfield register. Greenfield, green belt and unassessed land are absent.
  • Sites must be ≥0.25 ha or ≥5 dwellings to appear at all. Small infill is invisible here.
  • Capacity figures are the councils' own estimates, not independent assessments.
  • "Not started" is really "not recorded as started." The register's own Started flag appears on just 49 London sites, so this leans on end-date as a completion proxy. It is a proxy and we call it one.
  • Permission status needed cleaning. The register specifies three values; London rows contain twelve, including free text and typos. Every remap is logged and auditable.
  • 276 of 5,185 sites have no usable coordinates — counted in every total, absent from the map.

Three signals, ranked together

The ledger ranks boroughs on stalled capacity alone. This combines three: stalled capacity as a share of target, demand pressure, and the absolute number of homes held up. Each is percentile-ranked and averaged, so every borough that publishes capacity gets a score.

The score and the badge answer different questions, and are kept separate on purpose. The score is an average — a borough can rank well on it while being mediocre on one axis. The ● badge is the stricter claim: this borough clears the London median on all three axes independently. 5 of 24 do.

Borough Index All three % of target Demand pressure Homes stalled

Index is the mean of three percentile ranks across the 24 boroughs that publish capacity. The 9 boroughs flagged n/p are unscored — a borough that does not publish capacity is missing, not low-priority, and never scores zero. ⚑ marks partial capacity or a low site count: the score stands, but rests on thinner counts.

Who misses the badge, and on what

These boroughs clear two of the three axes and fail the third — they score, sometimes highly, but do not earn the badge. In almost every case the axis they fail is demand: they have the land and the permissions, but market pressure sits below the London median. Whether that argues for deprioritising them or for building where land is actually available is a political question, not a statistical one.

How to read this — and how not to

  • A high index is not "build here". It means three independent signals agree this borough is worth a planner's attention first. The decision still needs local knowledge this dataset does not contain.
  • A missing score is not a low score. The 9 boroughs that publish no capacity are absent from this table entirely. They are not at the bottom of it — they are not in it, and that is a gap in what councils publish, not a finding about those boroughs.
  • An average can hide a weak axis. Barking & Dagenham scores 69.4 — fifth — on the strength of capacity and raw homes, while its demand sits in the bottom 15%. That is exactly what the badge is for.
  • The threshold is a choice, not a fact. It is set at the median. Moving it would change who qualifies — at a stricter cut the list shortens toward Kensington & Chelsea alone. Waltham Forest sits exactly on the line at the 50th percentile for demand: it qualifies here, and would drop out under any stricter cut. Read the bottom of this table as provisional.
  • Barking & Dagenham is the case to argue about. It holds the largest stalled permitted area in London and scores nothing here, because demand sits in the bottom 5%. Whether that is a reason to deprioritise it or the opposite is a political question, not a statistical one.
